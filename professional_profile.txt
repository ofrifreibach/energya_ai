MASTER'S THESIS

Title: "From Financial Incentives to Installation: The Economics of Solar Adoption in Israel"

Institution: The Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Degree: Research-based MA in Economics, 2022-2025

Thesis grade: 95

Advisor: Professor Alon Eizenberg

Research question

The thesis asks how upfront PV installation costs and the expected future revenue created by Israel's changing feed-in-tariff schemes affect the probability that a potential adopter installs a photovoltaic system. It also quantifies the fiscal costs and social benefits of a marginal increase in the tariff for selected system-size segments.

Why the question matters

Israel has abundant solar resources and relies primarily on PV for renewable electricity, but adoption has been uneven even when projects appear financially attractive. Policymakers therefore need to know whether changing financial incentives actually induces additional installations, which market segments respond most strongly, and how much public or consumer-funded support is paid to projects that would have been installed anyway. The thesis connects private investment behavior to the public-policy objective of expanding renewables while controlling the excess cost of support schemes.

Data construction

Ofri built an individual-level panel covering Israel's current and potential dual-use rooftop PV installations from 2008 through 2025. The empirical models use 18,881,908 individual-year observations. The CV summarizes the underlying data as covering approximately 1.3 million rooftops and 50,000 PV adopters.

The dataset combines:

- administrative records of grid-connected PV installations;
- detailed feed-in-tariff regulations and tariff schedules;
- estimates of PV installation costs over time and by system size;
- GIS-based information on buildings and rooftop areas;
- municipality-level grid, built-environment, and socioeconomic variables.

Potential rooftop capacity was estimated from building area and assumptions about usable roof area by building type. A Ministry of Energy benchmark of roughly 7 square meters per kW was used. Usable-roof shares differed by building category: 20% for residential buildings, 70% for industrial buildings, and 40% for other buildings such as education, health, and government facilities. Installed systems were reconciled with the potential-building population to avoid double counting.

Each potential adopter appears once per year until adoption. Adoption is treated as a terminal event: after installing, the adopter exits the risk set. Non-adopters are observed through 2025 and are right-censored. The model focuses on the extensive margin - whether to install a technically feasible system now or wait - rather than jointly estimating the optimal system size and installation timing.

Financial model inside the thesis

For every rooftop-year observation, Ofri constructed measures of expected project economics under the tariff and cost conditions relevant to that rooftop, system size, regulation, and year.

The benchmark assumptions include a 25-year economic lifetime, a 6% real annual discount rate, first-year generation of 1,700 kWh per installed kW, annual generation degradation of 0.6%, and annual operating and maintenance costs equal to 1.7% of upfront investment cost.

The principal financial measures are:

- Upfront cost: technically feasible system size multiplied by the applicable cost per kW.
- Discounted net benefits: the present value of annual feed-in-tariff revenue minus operating and maintenance costs over the system lifetime.
- Net present value (NPV): discounted net benefits minus upfront cost.
- Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE): the present value of capital cost divided by the present value of lifetime electricity production. In this thesis, O&M remains on the benefit side to preserve a clean distinction between upfront costs and future net flows.
- Average discounted benefit per kWh: discounted net benefits divided by discounted lifetime production.
- Markup: average discounted benefit per kWh minus LCOE. This is equivalent to NPV per discounted kWh and represents the policy-created profitability margin. A positive markup corresponds to positive NPV, zero is the break-even point, and a negative markup indicates that the project is financially unattractive under the benchmark assumptions.

The markup is especially useful because it reduces a complex stream of changing tariffs, degradation, costs, and operating expenses to one comparable NIS-per-kWh measure across systems, years, sizes, and regulatory regimes.

Econometric approach

The adoption decision is modeled in a random-utility, binary discrete-choice framework. In each year, a potential adopter chooses between installing and not installing. The observed component of the utility difference depends on the relevant financial-return measure, municipality and policy controls, system-size segment, and year effects.

Ofri estimated linear probability models and pooled probit models. Linear probability models made it computationally feasible to estimate multiple specifications, high-dimensional interactions, and year fixed effects on nearly 19 million observations. Their limitations are acknowledged: fitted probabilities can lie outside zero and one, marginal effects are constant, and errors are heteroskedastic. The pooled probit model was estimated for the markup specification. Average marginal effects were reported so results could be interpreted as changes in adoption probability. Standard errors were clustered by municipality to account for correlation within municipalities over time.

Four complementary specifications were used:

1. upfront cost and discounted future benefits entered separately;
2. LCOE and average discounted benefits per kWh entered separately;
3. NPV as one combined profitability measure;
4. markup as a per-kWh profitability measure.

Financial measures were interacted with regulatory system-size bins: under 15 kW, 15-100 kW, 100-200 kW, 200-300 kW, 300-630 kW, and above 630 kW. This allows the relationship between profitability and adoption to differ across segments with different owners, regulations, financing constraints, and grid or permitting barriers.

Controls included a proxy for grid-connectivity conditions, eligibility for an expedited connection track, eligibility for an urban tariff premium, population density, wages, homeownership, and urban-renewal activity. Year fixed effects absorb common national shocks such as macroeconomic changes, broad technology-cost trends, and nationwide policy changes.

Main findings

For systems up to 100 kW, the results consistently show that higher expected returns and lower costs are associated with higher adoption. This pattern is robust across the alternative financial measures and both the linear probability and probit approaches.

For the under-15 kW and 15-100 kW segments, adoption was not substantially more responsive to an equivalent reduction in upfront cost than to an increase in discounted future benefits. The thesis therefore finds no clear evidence, within this framework, that potential adopters are disproportionately focused on upfront cost or strongly short-sighted about future tariff income.

In the year-fixed-effects probit model, a 0.01 NIS/kWh increase in markup was associated with an estimated increase in annual adoption probability of approximately 0.004 percentage points for systems under 15 kW and 0.022 percentage points for systems of 15-100 kW.

For systems above 100 kW, estimates were unstable and sometimes negative. The thesis explicitly warns against interpreting this as evidence that higher profitability causes lower adoption. A more plausible explanation is policy endogeneity: regulators raised tariffs in segments where adoption was already weak. These larger projects are also more exposed to unobserved grid, permitting, financing, and timing constraints. The results for these segments are therefore mainly descriptive and do not receive a clean causal interpretation.

Policy cost-effectiveness exercise

Ofri translated estimated marginal effects into concrete policy units: expected additional installations, installed capacity, lifetime electricity generation, additional feed-in-tariff payments, monetized social benefits, and net cost per induced kWh.

The key distinction is between payments on additional generation associated with induced adoption and higher payments on baseline generation that would have existed without the tariff increase.

Using 2022 as a reference year and a hypothetical 0.01 NIS/kWh tariff increase, the thesis estimated:

- Under 15 kW: about 37 additional installations, 366 kW of additional capacity, 14.7 GWh of additional lifetime generation, approximately NIS 10 million in incremental tariff payments, approximately NIS 6 million in social benefits, and a net cost of about NIS 0.26 per additional kWh.
- 15-100 kW: 1,391 kW of additional capacity, 55.6 GWh of additional lifetime generation, approximately NIS 91 million in incremental tariff payments, approximately NIS 24 million in social benefits, and a net cost of about NIS 1.22 per additional kWh.

The thesis contains an internal inconsistency in the 15-100 kW installation count: the prose reports 2,318 additional systems, while the reported capacity and representative system size imply about 23. Do not give an installation count for this segment unless the thesis is formally corrected by Ofri.

The social-benefit accounting includes avoided fuel costs, avoided investment in conventional power plants, reduced distribution and transmission requirements, avoided grid losses, avoided air-pollution and greenhouse-gas damages, and energy-security benefits.

The policy implication is not that the thesis derives a single optimal tariff. Rather, it provides a transparent, segment-specific framework for choosing where incentives are likely to induce more deployment relative to their cost. It shows why differentiated tariffs by system size can be more effective than a uniform increase and why policymakers must account for payments to existing generation as well as the additional renewable generation induced.

Important limitations and interpretation

- Tariffs were not randomly assigned. The regulator adjusted tariffs partly in response to observed costs and weak adoption, creating a policy-feedback problem.
- Individual fixed effects were not included, so unobserved time-invariant differences between potential adopters may remain.
- The model is static: it estimates annual adoption versus waiting but does not solve a full dynamic optimization problem.
- Technically feasible system size is treated as predetermined rather than jointly chosen with adoption timing.
- The 6% discount rate is a financial benchmark, not an estimate of each household's or firm's subjective discount rate. A higher rate would generally reduce NPV and markup and increase LCOE.
- Peer effects and other behavioral frictions are not separately identified.
- Identification is strongest for systems up to 100 kW. Results for systems above 100 kW are treated cautiously and are not given a clean causal interpretation.
